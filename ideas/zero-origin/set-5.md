Evidence note: failure modes below are derived from the stated Round 1 judging criteria
(problem identification/validation, affected users, innovative and feasible solution,
real-world impact), the 92-participant field, the no-build Round 1 format, and the
healthcare statement's explicit "coordination tool, not a diagnostic system" constraint.

Top 3 failure modes for this submission: (1) no validation — the first listed criterion
is clarity of problem identification/validation, and a deck without primary evidence
fails it by construction; (2) the infeasibility trap — "innovative and feasible" is
two-part, and an ML-heavy solution the team cannot prototype in Round 2's 10-day window
fails the second half; (3) generic-deck apathy — 92 entries, mostly AI-dashboard
fantasies, and the judges are students too.

1. **Two Weeks of Data Before the Deck** — removes no-validation.
   - Mechanism: before a single slide is written, the team collects two weeks of primary
     evidence from one real instance of the chosen problem (bin logs from the college
     kitchen, wait times at one local service window, ten student interviews), and slide
     two is that data.
   - Kill risk: two weeks of fieldwork against a 2026-09-26 deadline is a real cost — if
     the fieldwork fails, the deck has no spine.
2. **The 10-Day Contract** — removes the infeasibility trap.
   - Mechanism: the deck's final section is the Round 2 build plan cut to the exact
     2026-10-05 to 2026-10-14 window — features, owners, demo milestones — with every
     feature that does not fit ten days explicitly cut and named under "not in scope".
   - Kill risk: a visible cut list can read as small ambition next to "build the next
     frontier" decks; the deck must still carry one sharp innovation.
3. **Name the User on Slide One** — removes generic-deck apathy.
   - Mechanism: the pitch opens with one named affected user — one sentence of their
     week — before the problem category is even named, so the deck is the first of 92
     the judges hear that starts from a person instead of a dashboard.
   - Kill risk: one user cannot carry the impact criterion; the generalization has to
     land within the next two slides or the deck looks anecdotal.
4. **Anti-pitch: provably not the dashboard fantasy** — the submission judges do not
   want is a 40-slide AI-dashboard deck with no named user, no primary data, a
   "real-time ML" architecture the team cannot build in ten days, and (for the healthcare
   statement) a diagnostic system that violates the statement's own "coordination tool,
   not a diagnostic system" line; the smallest design provably not that is a 15-slide
   deck with data on slide two, a named user on slide one, the 10-day build plan as the
   final section, and the coordination-not-diagnosis constraint quoted verbatim as the
   scope definition.
   - Kill risk: 15 dense slides can feel thin beside 40 light ones; every slide must
     earn its place or the terseness reads as emptiness.
