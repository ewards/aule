Evidence note: the 10 problem statements are single clauses in the problem file; the
hard requirements below come from the stated Round 1 format and judging criteria, and
no per-statement detail is assumed beyond what the file says.

Hard requirements (Round 1, the deadline this run): one of the 10 statements (or a
validated Open-Innovation problem within the five themes); solution PPT + pitch video on
Devpost by 2026-09-26; team of 2-4 students; judging on clarity of problem
identification/validation, understanding of affected users, innovative and feasible
solution, potential real-world impact. No build is required until Round 2 (paid,
10-day prototype window).

Inherited assumptions checked: an ML model is required, a national-scale story, a
polished architecture diagram, that the team already knows the affected users, that a
big product beats a small one, that the deck must show a product.

1. **One Kitchen, One Number, One Deck** — pick the food-waste statement for exactly one
   kitchen (the team's own college kitchen): two weeks of real bin logs, the weekly
   "order less of this" list as the whole system, and a per-kitchen unit-economics slide
   for scale.
   - Discards: ML model, multi-tenant SaaS, app store, national narrative.
   - Minimal yet judge-proof: criteria 1 and 2 (problem validation, user understanding)
     are answered with primary data almost no competing team will have, and the
     solution is small enough to be credible for a 10-day Round 2 build.
   - Kill risk: the impact criterion wants scale; one kitchen must generalize via the
     unit-economics slide or it stays small.
2. **The Pilot Letter** — the deck includes a signed (or on-video) letter of intent from
   one real institution to pilot the chosen solution — the college kitchen, a hospital
   front desk, a local bank branch — replacing the TAM slide as the impact proof.
   - Discards: TAM modelling, the "addressable market" fantasy.
   - Minimal yet judge-proof: "potential real-world impact" is answered by a concrete
     adoption path, which is the hardest criterion for a student deck to fake and the
     easiest to prove.
   - Kill risk: a student-collected letter can read as a formality; the institution must
     be named specifically and the person on video must be able to say what they will
     pilot.
3. **Ten Interviews Before the Solution Slide** — restructure the deck so the first
   third is ten named, interviewed users (with quotes), and the solution starts only
   after them; the assumption being discarded is that the team speaks for users it has
   not met.
   - Discards: the feature list, the architecture diagram, the persona-invented-from-
     empathy.
   - Minimal yet judge-proof: criteria 1-2 are the first two listed criteria, and
     interview quotes are the cheapest evidence a student team can produce before
     2026-09-26.
   - Kill risk: ten quick interviews can be shallow; a quote that sounds researched
     rather than heard will do more harm than none.
4. **The 10-Day Prototype Claim (redefinition)** — naive reading: pick a statement and
   design a tech solution; minimal reading: prove that one real problem, one place, one
   user group, can be prototyped in Round 2's exact window. The deck's spine is the
   build plan for 2026-10-05 to 2026-10-14, cut feature by feature to fit.
   - Discards: the product fantasy, the multi-phase roadmap, anything not buildable in
     ten days by two-to-four students.
   - Minimal yet judge-proof: "innovative and feasible" is a two-part criterion and the
     deck answers the feasibility half with a verifiable plan instead of an adjective.
   - Kill risk: Round 2 is optional and paid; if the team does not advance, the 10-day
     claim is slides about slides — the deck must stand as a solution on its own.
